# M2 — Market Positioning

## What this block decides

The place this product occupies in the buyer's mind relative to everything else they could do, and the strategy used to claim it. Positioning is a choice about what to give up.

## What this block is not

- Not messaging. Messaging is `M4` and `M7`. Positioning is the structural claim messaging must serve.
- Not a competitive matrix. A matrix compares; positioning chooses which axis matters.
- Not aspiration. If the product cannot defend the position under scrutiny, it is not a position, it is a wish.

## Required sections

1. **Positioning strategy.** One of four, chosen explicitly: **competition** (better on a dimension the buyer already uses), **complementarity** (fits alongside what they have), **superiority** (a different class of solution), **innovation or category creation** (a new frame). Each has a different cost, and the cheapest defensible one usually wins.
2. **Why not the other three.** What each would require in budget, time, and proof. This is where over-positioning is caught: category creation chosen because it feels ambitious, priced as if it were free.
3. **Competitive frame.** Who the buyer compares against, in the order they come to mind. Include the status quo from `P1` and the in-house build. The frame is the buyer's, not the company's.
4. **The axis.** The single dimension on which this product wins. One. A product that wins on four dimensions has not chosen and will be remembered for none.
5. **What is conceded.** The dimensions where competitors are better, stated plainly. Positioning that concedes nothing is not believed.
6. **The conceptual enemy.** The belief, practice, or default the product argues against — not usually a company. The enemy is what makes the narrative in `M7` have somewhere to go.
7. **Punchlines.** Three to five compressed statements that carry the position, each falsifiable and defensible. Not slogans: claims. Each paired with the proof obligation it creates.
8. **Proof obligations.** What must be demonstrable for each punchline to survive contact with a skeptical buyer, and whether it exists today. A punchline whose proof does not exist is a liability handed to sales.
9. **Objections this position creates.** Every position creates predictable objections. Enumerate them here so `V3` can prepare rather than improvise.
10. **Buyer education required.** Given the awareness distribution in `P3`, how much belief change is needed before the position lands, and who pays for that: content in `M7`, sales time in `V2`, or partner credibility in `M5`.
11. **Defensibility.** How long this position holds if the strongest competitor decides to contest it, and what would make it stick longer.

## Evidence rules by regime

- **Greenfield.** Positioning against the status quo is usually stronger than against a named competitor, because the status quo cannot respond.
- **Extension.** Positioning must not contradict the parent product's. State the relationship: extension of the same claim, or a deliberate separation.
- **Repositioning.** Name what the market currently believes and what specifically will change it. Repositioning fails most often when the message moves and the product does not — if `P2` did not change, say so here rather than discovering it after launch.

## Quality tests

- Is the chosen strategy the cheapest one that is defensible, or the most flattering one available?
- Does section 5 concede anything a competitor would agree is true?
- Does every punchline have a proof obligation, and does that proof exist?
- Would the strongest competitor find this position hard to copy, or merely annoying?

## Probing questions

- What are you willing to be worse at?
- What does the buyer believe today that must stop being true?
- If a competitor announced your exact position next month, what would still be yours?
- Who pays for the education this position requires, and is it in a budget?

## Contradictions to watch

- Innovation or category creation chosen while `P2` describes replacing a known vendor → the honest strategy is competition, and the education budget was never approved.
- The winning axis is a feature `P2` marked as a short-lived lead → the position expires before the market learns it.
- The position requires proof `P2` section 8 says does not exist yet.
