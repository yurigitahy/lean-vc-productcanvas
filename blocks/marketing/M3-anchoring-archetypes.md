# M3 — Anchoring Archetypes

## What this block decides

The character the brand plays in the buyer's story, chosen to fit how the persona in `P4` actually behaves and to carry the position in `M2`. Two archetypes: one primary, one secondary. The primary sets the default register; the secondary handles what the primary cannot.

## What this block is not

- Not a brand personality exercise for internal enjoyment. If the archetype does not change a real decision about how something is written or shown, it was decoration.
- Not chosen from what the company admires about itself.
- Not a substitute for tone of voice. `M4` converts these into rules.

## Required sections

1. **Primary archetype.** Named, with the reason it fits the persona's situation rather than the company's self-image. Explain what this archetype promises the buyer emotionally, alongside what `P2` promises them functionally.
2. **Secondary archetype.** What the primary cannot cover. Common pattern: a primary that carries authority and a secondary that carries approachability, or the reverse.
3. **Fit to persona.** For each persona in `P4`, whether the primary lands, and where it does not. A single archetype rarely serves an economic buyer and a hands-on user identically.
4. **Fit to position.** How the archetype makes the `M2` position more credible. A superiority position with a self-effacing archetype undermines itself; an innovation position with a bureaucratic one does too.
5. **Behavioral implications.** What the archetype means concretely: how the product speaks when it fails, how it handles a price increase, what it never jokes about, whether it explains or asserts, whether it leads with evidence or with vision.
6. **Visual and structural implications.** Density, formality, use of numbers, use of imagery, naming conventions. Enough for a designer to make decisions without asking.
7. **Anti-archetypes.** What the brand must never read as. Usually more actionable than the positive definition. Includes the archetype the category defaults to, if the position is to differ from it.
8. **Where the archetype breaks.** The situations in which holding the character costs credibility — an outage, a security incident, a regulatory question — and what supersedes it there.
9. **Consistency test.** A short set of decisions someone could make correctly using only this block: a subject line, a refusal, a delay notification.

## Evidence rules by regime

- **Greenfield.** The archetype is a bet. Name the category default it departs from and why the departure is worth the friction.
- **Extension.** The archetype should be compatible with the parent brand unless separation is intentional. State which.
- **Repositioning.** The market already has an impression. Name it, and state whether the new archetype is a shift or a correction — corrections require acknowledging the old one.

## Quality tests

- Could two people, using only this block, write the same refusal email in tone?
- Does the archetype serve the buyer's self-image, or the company's?
- Is there an anti-archetype that the company currently resembles?
- Does the archetype survive bad news?

## Probing questions

- When the product fails, does the brand apologize, explain, or take responsibility? Those are three different archetypes.
- What does the category sound like by default, and are you joining it or leaving it?
- Which archetype would your most skeptical buyer find insulting?

## Contradictions to watch

- The archetype fits the company's self-image but not the persona's behavior in `P4` section 3 → tone the buyer does not respond to.
- The archetype undercuts the `M2` strategy → position and character are pulling against each other; one must move.
