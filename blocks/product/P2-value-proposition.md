# P2 — Value Proposition

## What this block decides

What the product is, what it promises, and by what mechanism it delivers that promise — in terms the buyer can evaluate without trusting the seller. `P2` is the second most reused block in the canvas. Seven blocks read it.

## What this block is not

- Not a feature list. Features are how the promise is kept; they belong in the mechanism section and nowhere else.
- Not a slogan. `M1` writes the pitch. `P2` decides what the pitch is allowed to claim.
- Not a comparison table. Differentiation here means the axis on which comparison happens, not the scorecard.
- Not aspiration. Everything claimed here must be deliverable at launch, or explicitly staged with dates.

## Required sections

1. **What the product is.** The literal object: software, service, marketplace, device, hybrid. One sentence a procurement officer could file.
2. **Core promise.** The change in the customer's situation, stated as an outcome, tied directly to the top of the pain hierarchy in `P1`. One sentence. If it takes two, the promise is two promises and one of them is secondary — say which.
3. **Mechanism.** How the promise is kept. This is the section buyers use to decide whether to believe the promise. Vague mechanism is a common cause of long sales cycles: the buyer cannot evaluate the claim, so they delay.
4. **Economic value created.** In the customer's money: revenue gained, cost removed, risk priced, or time converted. State the assumptions in the calculation. This is the ceiling `P5` prices against.
5. **What it replaces or displaces.** The budget line, the vendor, the process, or the headcount. Every purchase displaces something, including "doing nothing," which has its own cost.
6. **Substitutes and alternatives.** Direct competitors, adjacent tools, in-house builds, and the status quo. For each: what it does better, and what it structurally cannot do.
7. **Differentiation and its durability.** What is hard to copy, and how long it holds. Distinguish a feature lead (months), an integration or data position (years), and a structural advantage (indefinite). Overstating durability here produces a positioning in `M2` that collapses on contact.
8. **Proof available today.** Cases, benchmarks, references, demos, guarantees, pilots. What can actually be shown to a skeptical buyer this quarter. Sales in `V5` can only use what exists.
9. **What the product deliberately does not do.** Scope boundaries stated as choices. Missing boundaries produce a sales team that promises everything and a CS team that inherits it.
10. **Staged promise.** If part of the promise depends on unbuilt capability, what is true at launch versus in six and twelve months. Anything unstaged is assumed by everyone downstream to be true today.
11. **The one-sentence customer test.** What the customer would say changed in their business after adoption. If nobody can complete that sentence yet, the value proposition is not finished.

## Evidence rules by regime

- **Greenfield.** The economic value figure is a model, not a measurement. Show the model and its assumptions.
- **Extension.** Proof should include existing product performance where the mechanism is shared. Say which proof transfers and which does not.
- **Repositioning.** Write sections 2 and 3 twice: promised today, promised after. Where they differ, name what in the product changes to justify the new claim. If nothing changes, the repositioning is a message change and `M2` must be told.

## Quality tests

- Could a competitor's marketing page make the same core promise? If yes, the promise is category-level and differentiation has not been decided.
- Does the mechanism explain *why* the promise holds, or only *that* it holds?
- Does the economic value in section 4 exceed the likely price by at least three times? Below that, price becomes the whole conversation in `V3`.
- Is anything in the promise dependent on the customer doing work? Say so — it becomes onboarding scope in `C3` and a churn risk in `C8`.

## Canonical extract

```
P2 — Value Proposition
What it is:          <literal object, one sentence>
Core promise:        <outcome, tied to P1's top pain>
Mechanism:           <why the promise holds>
Economic value:      <customer-side figure and its basis>
Replaces:            <budget line, vendor, process, or nothing>
Main substitutes:    <up to three, with what each cannot do>
Differentiation:     <what is hard to copy> ; durable for <horizon>
Not in scope:        <explicit boundaries>
Staged:              <what is only true later, and when>
```

## Probing questions

- If the customer already had budget approved, what would still make them hesitate?
- Which part of the promise would you drop if you had to launch in half the time?
- What can a competitor say about you that is true and unflattering?
- Who has to change how they work for the promise to be kept?

## Contradictions to watch

- The promise addresses a symptom from `P1` section 2 rather than the core problem → the product is a painkiller sold as a cure; `M2` positioning will be harder than expected.
- Differentiation described as a feature but claimed as durable → expect `M2` to over-position and `V3` to face objections it cannot answer.
- The mechanism requires a capability owned by another product → that product is missing from `P6`.
