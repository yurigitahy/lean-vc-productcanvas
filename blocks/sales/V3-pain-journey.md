# V3 — Pain Journey

## What this block decides

How the conversation moves a buyer from their current framing of their situation to a decision — the questions that surface pain, the arguments that reframe it, and the objections that arrive on the way.

## What this block is not

- Not a script. `V4` sequences this into one.
- Not a list of product benefits. Benefits are answers; this block is mostly about questions.
- Not objection rebuttals in the debating sense. Objections are information; the block distinguishes those that must be resolved from those that mask a different concern.

## Required sections

1. **The pain narrative.** How the buyer describes their situation before the product is mentioned, in their words. Drawn from `P1` section 2 and `P4` section 9. Sellers who cannot say this sentence do not get to say anything else.
2. **Discovery questions by persona.** For each persona in `P4`, the questions that surface the `P1` problem without leading. Ordered from safe to sharp. A discovery question the buyer can answer without thinking is not doing work.
3. **The conflict.** The contradiction in the buyer's current situation that they have not resolved: paying for something twice, growing in a way that increases dependency, measuring something that does not predict what they need. This is the moment the conversation turns.
4. **Triggers to listen for.** Statements that indicate an urgency trigger from `P1` section 8 is active. Sellers detect urgency; they do not create it.
5. **Arguments, ranked.** The three to five that move buyers, strongest first, each tied to what the persona is measured on in `P4` section 4. Include what each argument requires as proof.
6. **Reframes.** Where the buyer's framing must change for the purchase to make sense: from cost to investment, from tool to capability, from department to company problem. Each reframe with the question that opens it rather than the statement that asserts it.
7. **Objections, categorized.** Price, timing, trust, incumbent, internal politics, technical fit, and prior bad experience. For each: what it usually means underneath, what resolves it, and what it looks like when it is unresolvable. Distinguish objections that are decisions from objections that are requests for help.
8. **Objections the position creates.** From `M2` section 9. Choosing a position generates predictable resistance; this is where the company decides to answer it well.
9. **Competitive responses.** What each named competitor says about the company, and the honest response. Dishonest responses lose the deal later.
10. **The no-decision case.** Doing nothing is the most common competitor. What makes staying put feel safe, and what makes it feel expensive, per `P1` section 6.
11. **Landmines.** What a seller can say that ends a deal: an unproven claim from `M1`'s forbidden list, a promise about the staged roadmap in `P2`, a dismissal of an incumbent the buyer chose.

## Evidence rules by regime

- **Greenfield.** Objections are anticipated, not observed. Mark them as such, and set a review after the first twenty conversations.
- **Extension.** Reuse the objection log from existing products for anything sharing the buyer or the budget line.
- **Repositioning.** The strongest objection is often the buyer's memory of the old positioning. Address it directly rather than hoping it does not come up.

## Quality tests

- Can a seller run discovery for ten minutes without naming the product?
- Does every argument connect to a metric a persona is measured on?
- Is there an objection with no good answer? Say so — sales will find it anyway, and knowing which deals to leave is worth more than a rebuttal.
- Does the no-decision case get as much attention as the competitive one?

## Probing questions

- What does the buyer say in the first two minutes of a call that tells you this will close?
- Which objection do your sellers handle badly, consistently?
- What does the buyer's boss ask them after your meeting?
- Which deals should you walk away from earlier than you do?

## Contradictions to watch

- Triggers and arguments have no root in `P1` → invented at the desk; they will not survive contact.
- Objections created by the `M2` position are missing here → sales improvises against resistance the company chose to create.
- The tone of the discovery questions contradicts `M4` → the conversation sounds like a different company than the content.
